1. Executive Summary

Fernbrook Pantry has a real product. Small-batch infused olive oils, spice blends, and jam, priced between $12 and $38, photographed well and written about with genuine care. That is not the problem. The problem is that the store asks a first-time visitor to trust it while showing her, in the first screen, a discount that does not exist and a hero product she cannot buy. Everything after that first impression is uphill.

The store's shape, as built for this sample: eleven products, traffic from Instagram and a little Google search, visits every day, and a trickle of orders. That gap, visits against orders, is the whole story of this report. None of the five findings below requires a redesign, a new theme, or a bigger ad budget. All five are presentation and paperwork: prices that say what they mean, a hero that can be bought, proof on the page, and two policy pages that do not exist yet.

The five findings that matter most:

  • The sale is not a sale. The best-selling Smoked Paprika Oil shows a regular price of $24.00 and a sale price of $24.00, under a red Sale badge. The pattern repeats across the grid: $18.00 and $18.00, $32.00 and $32.00. A visitor reads that in about a second, and what she learns is that this store stages discounts. In StoreClinic's crawl of 1,008 real Shopify stores, a sale price identical to the regular price appeared on 270 stores. It is the most common trust leak we log.
  • The hero product is sold out. The Wild Garlic Finishing Oil holds the big homepage slot, and every variant of it is sold out. Fernbrook features it because it photographs beautifully and it used to sell. In the same crawl, 104 of 1,008 stores had a featured product showing as sold out. A paid click lands on the prettiest product in the store, and the product cannot be bought.
  • There are no reviews anywhere. No stars, no quotes, no "my second bottle" on any product page. In the crawl, 521 of 1,008 stores had no reviews on the product page, more than half. Food is a trust purchase, and on Fernbrook's pages the only voice is the seller's.
  • There is no shipping page. What delivery costs, and how long a gift order takes, cannot be learned before the last step of checkout. In the crawl, 593 of 1,008 stores had no shipping page we could find.
  • There is no returns page. A buyer sending food as a gift cannot learn what happens if the jar arrives broken. In the crawl, 475 of 1,008 stores had no returns page. Plenty of buyers solve that uncertainty the cheap way: they close the tab.

2. Store Snapshot: What Fernbrook Is Selling

Eleven products, one idea: small-batch pantry goods that make weeknight cooking feel considered. The ladder is short and sensible. It is how the ladder is presented, and what is missing around it, that leaks money.

ProductPriceRole in the ladder
Spice blends and jam (Smoked Sea Salt Blend, Harissa Blend, Blackberry Sage Jam, and one more)$12–$16Entry / first purchase and gift add-ons
Individual infused olive oils (Smoked Paprika, Wild Garlic, Lemon Thyme, Chili Crunch, Rosemary, and one more)$22–$32Core range — the Smoked Paprika Oil at $24 is the best-seller
The Weeknight Duo (any two oils, boxed)$38Gift set / highest ticket in the store

What is working: the ladder has a genuine entry point under $15, a core range a home cook can browse without sticker shock, and a giftable top rung. The product names do real work — Smoked Paprika Oil tells a shopper what it is and what dinner it improves — and the photography is strong enough that the store chose its hero shot for beauty. That instinct is right; the hero product is wrong.

What is missing: an honest price story. Every anchor in the store is fake, because every compare-at price equals the price. There is no sum-of-parts on the Duo, so $38 has nothing to stand against. There is no proof, no shipping answer, and no returns answer anywhere a visitor can reach before checkout. The store is asking for trust at exactly the points where it provides the least evidence.

A note on the traffic: Fernbrook's visits come mostly from Instagram, which means most visitors arrive on a phone, mid-scroll, with about the patience you would expect. A store living on that traffic cannot afford a first screen that spends trust. It has one thumb-stop to be believed.

4. Product Page Copy Diagnosis

We reviewed the two pages that carry this store: the best-seller's product page and the homepage hero, which is doing product-page work for a product that cannot be bought.

Smoked Paprika Oil — $24 (the best-seller)

What works: the name, and a description that talks about what the oil does to eggs, potatoes, and white beans instead of how it is manufactured. A cook can picture dinner. That is rarer than it should be.

What is costing sales:

  • Its own card lies about its price. The product grid shows $24.00 struck against $24.00 with a Sale badge. The best-seller is the page most visitors reach, and its first message is a staged discount.
  • No proof on the page. No reviews, no star count, no single quoted customer. For the store's most popular product, that absence is the loudest thing on the page.
  • No shipping or breakage answer. Nothing on the page says what delivery costs, how fast it arrives, or what happens if a glass bottle breaks in transit, and no other page answers it either.

Paste-ready fix — open the page with the outcome, then close the open questions

"The bottle that makes Tuesday dinner taste deliberate. Cold-smoked paprika in a smooth olive oil: eggs, roast potatoes, white beans, and grilled bread all get better for it. 250 ml, about a month of weeknight cooking. Ships in 1–2 business days. If it arrives broken, we replace it, no photo of the damage required, no return shipping. That is the whole policy."

Homepage hero — Wild Garlic Finishing Oil (sold out)

What works: the photograph. It is the best image in the store, and the instinct to lead with beauty is correct for a food brand.

What is costing sales:

  • The product cannot be bought. Every variant is sold out. The hero is advertising a memory. Paid and social traffic land here first, so the store's highest-traffic slot earns nothing.
  • No capture on the way out. A visitor who wanted the Wild Garlic Oil gets a dead end: no "tell me when it is back," no suggestion of the closest in-stock oil. Her visit ends, and the store keeps nothing from it.
  • The grid below repeats the fake-sale pattern, so even a visitor who scrolls past the dead hero meets the staged discounts next.

Paste-ready fix — hero copy for the in-stock best-seller taking the slot

"Smoked Paprika Oil. Our best-seller, in stock, ships in 1–2 business days. The one bottle home cooks reorder."

And on the Wild Garlic page itself: "Back with the spring bottling. Leave your email and we will tell you the day it lands. Until then, Lemon Thyme is the closest pour."

5. Trust & Credibility Gap Analysis

Fernbrook's buyer is a home cook, often buying food as a gift, from a store she found mid-scroll. She is not hostile. She is one inconsistency away from closing the tab. These are the gaps she can find today, in the order she is likely to find them:

#GapWhat she seesFix

2Sold-out heroThe homepage's featured product cannot be bought, in any variant. In our crawl, 104 of 1,008 stores featured a sold-out product.Give the hero slot to the in-stock best-seller; move Wild Garlic to a back-in-stock page with an email field (Action 2).

The good news: every gap in that table is a copy fix or a settings change, not a rebuild. Fernbrook's underlying offer (small batches, fair prices, a product people reorder) is genuinely sellable. Trust is rarely about having a better product. It is about the buyer never catching you presenting the store two ways at once: premium in the photography, careless in the prices.

6. The One Thing

If you do only one thing from this report, do this:

Clear the fake sale, everywhere, today. For every product where the sale price and the regular price are the same number, set the regular price to the true original price if the product is genuinely discounted, or clear the compare-at price entirely and let it sell at one honest number. Then remove the Sale badge from any card where the two prices match.

Why this, over everything else:

  • It is on every surface. The sold-out hero wastes the homepage slot, but the fake sale is on every product card, every collection, every search result. It is the highest-surface-area leak in the store, and it is live right now, on the best-seller first.
  • It spends trust the store has not earned. A missing shipping page is an absence; a shopper may not notice it until checkout. A staged discount is a statement, and she notices it in the first second. It teaches her to doubt the $38 Duo, the batch story, and everything else the store says.
  • It costs nothing and needs nothing new. Eleven products, about twenty minutes in the product list. No copy to write, no photos to shoot, no app to install. The prices themselves do not move; nothing gets cheaper or more expensive. What changes is that the first thing a stranger reads is true.
  • It makes everything after it measurable. Reviews, a shipping page, a working hero: each lands better on a store whose prices can be believed, and each becomes easier to judge once the numbers on the page mean what they say.

Everything else in this report supports this move: the hero swap gives the honest prices a product that can actually be bought, the two policy pages answer the questions an honest price raises, and the review ask starts building the proof the store should have had all along.

A final word. Stores at Fernbrook's stage usually fail on product or on story. This one fails on neither. It fails on presentation: a staged discount here, a dead hero there, two missing pages, and proof that was never asked for. Presentation is the cheapest layer to fix, and once it is fixed, the product Fernbrook already has gets to do its work.
